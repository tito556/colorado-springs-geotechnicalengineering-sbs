At 6,035 feet of elevation with the Front Range as a backdrop, Colorado Springs presents foundation challenges that shallow spread footings simply cannot handle. The city’s underlying geology shifts dramatically within a single lot—from weathered Pierre Shale to decomposed Pikes Peak granite—and that transition zone is where differential settlement begins. A rigid raft foundation changes the math: instead of isolated pads fighting the soil independently, the entire mat acts as one unit, bridging soft spots and distributing column loads across a much larger footprint. For sites near the alluvial deposits along Fountain Creek or the expansive claystone prevalent in Briargate and Stetson Hills, we combine the mat design with a soil liquefaction assessment to verify that the bearing stratum remains stable under the design earthquake, then we validate compaction and moisture conditioning with Proctor testing before placing the mud slab. The result is a foundation system that works with Colorado Springs’ geology rather than against it.

A properly designed mat foundation does not eliminate soil movement—it manages it uniformly, turning differential heave into controlled rigid-body tilt that the structure can tolerate.

Technical details of the service in Colorado Springs

The most common mistake we encounter on Colorado Springs job sites is the assumption that a thicker slab automatically solves an expansive soil problem. Thickness adds stiffness, but without a properly engineered moisture barrier, drainage plan, and reinforcement layout, a 24-inch mat will heave just as unevenly as a 6-inch one—it just costs three times as much to demolish and replace. Effective raft design in this region demands a layered strategy:
  • Subgrade preparation that includes over-excavation of high-plasticity clays and replacement with select fill compacted to 95% of modified Proctor density
  • A capillary break and vapor barrier system that interrupts the moisture migration that drives seasonal volume change
  • Reinforcement detailing that accounts for both uniform bearing pressure and localized punching shear at column interfaces
We tie every design parameter back to laboratory index tests—Atterberg limits, grain-size distribution, and one-dimensional swell-consolidation curves—so that the structural engineer receives soil spring values rooted in actual site conditions, not regional averages. For projects where the bedrock surface slopes more than 10%, we integrate the mat design with an evaluation of slope stability to ensure that the building footprint does not compromise the long-term equilibrium of the hillside.

Risks and considerations in Colorado Springs

Consider two projects separated by just four miles: one in the older Broadmoor area, where medium-dense gravels overlie competent bedrock at a manageable depth, and another out east near the Banning Lewis Ranch, where thick sections of claystone swell 4% or more with seasonal moisture changes. The Broadmoor site might get by with isolated footings, but the Banning Lewis property needs a raft designed with a void form system beneath grade beams to absorb heave without cracking the superstructure. We see this contrast constantly in Colorado Springs — the difference between a straightforward geotechnical report and a costly structural repair often comes down to whether the mat thickness and reinforcement were tuned to the specific plasticity index of the on-site soils. A mat that is too flexible concentrates curvature under the heaviest columns; one that is too rigid wastes concrete and steel while still transferring movement to partition walls. Our approach quantifies the soil-structure interaction using subgrade reaction modulus values derived from in-situ plate load tests, not just textbook correlations, so the mat behaves predictably over the full design life of the building.

Our services

Every raft foundation we design in Colorado Springs initiates with a geotechnical investigation tailored to the building footprint and the mapped geologic unit at the site. Our services encompass the complete workflow, extending from subsurface exploration through construction-phase observation.

Mat Foundation Geotechnical Report

Soil borings to minimum 2x mat width depth, laboratory swell/consolidation testing, and derivation of modulus of subgrade reaction. Delivers bearing capacity, anticipated total and differential settlement, and recommendations for underslab drainage.

Structural Design Collaboration

We work directly with the structural engineer of record to translate soil spring values into the analysis model, review reinforcement layouts for temperature and shrinkage cracking, and check punching shear at concentrated loads.

Construction-Phase Testing & Observation

Proof-rolling observation, nuclear density testing of select fill lifts, rebar inspection prior to concrete placement, and slump/cylinder testing per ASTM C94. We document every lift so the as-built condition matches the design intent.

Quick answers

When does a Colorado Springs project need a raft foundation instead of isolated footings?

A raft typically becomes the better solution when the total column load exceeds 50% of the allowable bearing capacity under spread footings, or when the predicted differential settlement between adjacent footings is more than half an inch. In our local soils—particularly the expansive Pierre Shale and claystone units—a mat also reduces the risk of moisture-driven heave damage because the entire slab moves as one rigid body rather than allowing individual footings to tilt.

How do you verify that the subgrade under the mat will not settle over time?

We run one-dimensional consolidation tests (ASTM D2435) on undisturbed Shelby tube samples taken from the bearing stratum. The test yields the compression index and preconsolidation pressure, which we plug into a settlement analysis that accounts for the stress bulb depth beneath the mat. On sites with granular soils, we back-calculate settlement using SPT N-values corrected for overburden pressure and hammer energy per ASTM D1586.

Does a raft foundation eliminate the need for a frost-protected shallow foundation detail?

Not in Colorado Springs. The Pikes Peak Regional Building Department still requires a minimum 30-inch frost depth, and the mat edge must be protected either by vertical insulation extending down to that depth or by a thickened edge beam that bears below the frost line. We specify the detail based on the building occupancy classification and whether the structure will be heated year-round.
